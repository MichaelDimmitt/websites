# Planning Readiness: A Conversation

## Chapter 1: How Far Out Is Far Enough?

The conversation began with a simple question about timing — how many days out is best for planning? The answer depends entirely on what you're planning.

For **travel**, the sweet spot is 1–3 months out for domestic flights and 3–6 months for international. Hotels follow at 1–2 months, with peak seasons pushing everything 6 months or further. For **events**, venues need 6–12 months, vendors 3–6, and casual gatherings 2–4 weeks. **Appointments** vary by demand — routine doctors need 2–6 weeks, high-demand specialists can require 2–3 months, and the pro move is booking your next appointment before you leave the current one.

The governing principle:

> "The harder something is to change or the more other people depend on it, the earlier you should lock it in. Travel and events fall squarely in that category."

## Chapter 2: Demand Cycles and the Sliding Scale of Seasons

Planning isn't just about lead time — it's about timing your move to the low point of a demand cycle. Every market has a predictable rhythm, and savvy planners exploit the gap between supply becoming available and demand catching up.

The apartment rental example illustrated this perfectly: December through February is the dead zone for rentals. People don't want to move in winter, so landlords get motivated. Lease-end management software surfaces supply with no natural demand pressure — the window is right there to exploit.

The same pattern holds across categories:

- **Flights and hotels** — January and September sit right after the holiday and summer peaks, historically the cheapest months to book.
- **Event venues** — January through March and late fall offer significantly better rates outside of holidays.
- **Cruises and resorts** — shoulder seasons deliver the same product at a fraction of peak pricing.

The meta-strategy is flexibility on *when* rather than fixation on *a specific time*. The question becomes: what is the signal that tells you you're in the low-demand window?

## Chapter 3: Building a Personal Readiness System

The conversation shifted from general planning toward a personal operating system built around three specific needs.

**1. Travel**
Hard calendar events are the biggest asset. Work backwards from fixed return dates — lock the return the moment a hard deadline exists, then plan outbound with flexibility. This also prevents the most expensive scenario: last-minute one-way tickets home.

**2. Health Appointments — Yes, Seasonality Applies**
January and February are the worst months for medical availability. New year resolutions, new insurance kicking in, and deferred holiday appointments flood the system simultaneously. Late summer (August–September) has a similar crunch. The best windows are March–May and October–November.

**3. Hard Calendar Events as an Anchor System**
Fixed events are the skeleton. Everything else should be scheduled relative to them — travel buffers before and after, health appointments in calmer stretches between events, big purchases and projects in low-stress windows.

The framing is a **rolling horizon**: always looking 3–6 months out, with each category carrying its own lead time trigger. When something lands on the calendar, the question becomes: *what does this event require me to book right now?*

## Chapter 4: What Is an Acceptable Amount of Time to Take Off?

Norms around vacation length vary by context, but clear benchmarks exist.

**At work**, one week carries the least friction. Two weeks is accepted with 4–6 weeks of notice. Three or more weeks enters leave-of-absence territory.

**Internationally**, the frame shifts — Europeans routinely take 3–4 week holidays. For long-haul international travel, building in at least 10 days is widely recommended since travel eats 1–2 days on each end.

**Research on actual recovery** points consistently to 8–11 days as the window where people feel full psychological benefit. Shorter trips don't fully unwind, and longer ones don't proportionally add more benefit — the plateau arrives around day 8.

## Chapter 5: What Is a Leave of Absence?

A leave of absence (LOA) is an extended, formally approved period away from work that goes beyond routine vacation.

**Key distinctions from vacation:**
- Vacation is an expected benefit requiring only a manager's approval.
- LOA requires HR involvement and documentation.
- Vacation is typically paid; LOA can be paid, unpaid, or partially paid.

**Common reasons include** medical recovery, family care, parental leave, personal circumstances, education, and military service.

**Legal protections** in the US exist under FMLA (Family and Medical Leave Act), which guarantees up to 12 weeks unpaid for qualifying reasons at companies with 50 or more employees, with job protection throughout.

For practical planning purposes, LOA is primarily a boundary marker — vacations live comfortably below it, and knowing that threshold clarifies how much time can be taken without entering formal negotiation with an employer.

## Chapter 6: Running a Business Changes Everything

For business owners, there is no HR department, no formal approval process — and often, higher stakes.

The core challenge is that when you own the business, you *are* the system. Taking time off means the business pauses, slows, or someone else carries it — none of which happen automatically.

Most small business owners can step away for 1–2 weeks with preparation. Beyond that, cracks appear unless systems and people are deliberately in place. Many owners take fragmented time off — checking in remotely, never fully disconnecting — because true extended absence requires infrastructure built well in advance.

**The 30-day benchmark** is the standard most business advisors point to as the baseline of a healthy business: if the business cannot run without you for 30 days, the business owns you more than you own it.

Getting there requires three things:
1. Someone who can make decisions in your absence
2. Systems and processes that are documented
3. Clients and customers who can be served without you

The goal is not just rest — it's building a business that is more valuable, less stressful, and more scalable. The 30-day test is the measure of whether you've gotten there.
